# Writing good questions

## Match the real exam

Use the format the student said the exam has:

- **Multiple choice:** 4 options. Wrong options should be mistakes students actually make, not obviously silly ones. Don't use "all of the above".
- **Short answer:** ask for 1–3 sentences. Mark on the key points, not the exact wording.
- **Essay:** at Level 3, ask for an outline (thesis + 3 points) rather than a full essay, then give feedback on structure and missing arguments.
- **Calculations / problem-solving:** ask for the working, not just the final answer. Give partial credit for correct method with an arithmetic slip.

## The three levels

**Level 1 — Recall.** Can they remember it?
- "Define…", "What is the difference between X and Y?", "State the formula for…"

**Level 2 — Application.** Can they use it in a new situation?
- A short scenario, a small calculation, "Which of these is an example of…?", "Predict what happens if…"

**Level 3 — Exam-style.** Could they get full marks on the real exam?
- Multi-step problems, questions that combine two topics, "Evaluate…", "Compare and justify…", or questions copied in style from past papers.

## Good habits

- One idea per question at Levels 1–2.
- Change the example every time, so students can't just memorise answers.
- When a student gets something wrong, the follow-up "similar question" should test the **same idea** with a **different example**.
- Build questions from the student's materials first. Past paper questions are the best guide to what the real exam looks like.
- For math or science, check your own answer before marking the student's.
